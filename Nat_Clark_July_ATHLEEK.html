What We Measured
ATHLEEKAnalyseMetric Glossary
What the numbers mean
A plain-language guide to every metric in this report
Jump Height
CMJ Height. How high off the ground you jumped from a countermovement start. One of the most well-normed metrics in the report. Affected by technique and reactive, elastic qualities as much as by raw strength, so best read alongside power and reactive strength.
Reactive Strength
RSI Modified. Jump height divided by total movement time. Rewards both how high and how quickly you jump: a measure of spring and explosiveness. A key marker of elastic, reactive quality, and one of the most responsive metrics to spring-based training.
Explosive Power
Peak Power (rel.). The highest power output during the jump per kilogram of bodyweight. Can be elevated by a deep countermovement; best interpreted alongside jump height and RSI together rather than in isolation.
Leg Symmetry
Limb Asymmetry (CMJ). The percentage difference in force between left and right leg during the jump. Under 10% is considered well-balanced. A well-normed and practically meaningful metric: one of the most reliable results in this report.
Maximum Force
IMTP Peak Force. The maximum force produced during a maximal pull against a fixed bar, relative to bodyweight. A measure of overall lower body strength with good literature support. Responds well to structured strength training and is a highly trainable quality.
Rate of Force Development
RFD @ 200ms (IMTP). How quickly force rises in the first 200 milliseconds of a maximal pull. Captures neuromuscular speed and explosiveness. Presented as a descriptive category rather than a ranked band in this report because it is highly sensitive to testing setup, sampling rate, and onset detection: it is most useful as a longitudinal tracking metric over time.
Postural Stability
Sway Velocity. Average speed of the body's centre of pressure while standing still. Lower is better. Presented as a descriptive category: most useful as a longitudinal baseline and for fatigue or return-to-play monitoring. Lower readings indicate steadier balance and control.

This is a really positive session. Now that the equipment and movements are familiar, these numbers are a reliable read on where you are, and set against your March baseline almost everything has moved forward: your jump is higher, your symmetry and balance have improved, your rate of force development is up, and your maximal strength has pushed on further still. That is exactly the pattern you want to see between sessions.

The clear standout is your maximal strength. At 2.67 times bodyweight you are in the top 10% of active women your age, and you stay at the top even against competitive triathletes. For a runner, strength this high relative to bodyweight supports running economy, helps you hold form when you tire, and is linked to staying injury-free through a training block. Your leg symmetry is the other real positive: at 1.14% difference between left and right you are beautifully balanced, and even tighter than in March. That points to clean, even running mechanics and is a quality well worth protecting.

Where the biggest gains are

Your profile has a very clear shape: you produce plenty of force, but not yet very quickly. Your reactive strength, jump height and power all sit lower than your strength would suggest, and together they are the single biggest opportunity in front of you. This is genuinely good news, because these fast, reactive qualities are the most trainable of all, and you already have a strong force base to draw on: there is a lot of power waiting to be unlocked. It is also already moving, with your jump up from 12.2 cm to 16.8 cm since March, which shows the direction of travel is right.

A quick word on your power number. Your peak power in the jump was about 29 W/kg. Your March card showed 11.02 W/kg, but those measure different things: March reported your average power through the push, whereas peak power is always higher. Compared like for like on the average basis, your power has edged up from about 11 to about 13 W/kg since March. Because our comparison data for power is on the average basis, we have not attached a banded ranking to your peak figure here, and on a like-for-like basis power remains a development area, in line with your jump and reactive scores.

Rate of force development and postural sway are both shown as descriptive only, in line with our standard approach: they are most useful as baselines to track over time. Both improved on March, and your sway reading in particular is low and favourable, which reflects good balance and control.
